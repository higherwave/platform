Comparative analysis · Dependency visualization for the CIO

Finding the three things that matter in a 10,000-node org

Eight ways to draw a Salesforce integration and dependency graph, judged by how well each answers a CIO’s questions before a change ships.

From market research

Scale makes the map unreadable. A large Salesforce org can have thousands of fields and hundreds of automations. A complete map is useless if nobody can find the three things that matter for the change at hand. This is fundamentally a progressive-disclosure and scoping problem, which makes it squarely a design problem.

What the CIO needs from the map

What breaks if we change this?
The blast radius of a field, flow, or package before it ships.
What does this system touch?
The footprint of an integration like NetSuite or Marketo, for renewals and vendor replacement.
Where is risk concentrated?
Objects with overlapping automations, near platform limits, or without an owner.
Who needs to be in the room?
The teams and reports downstream of a change, so sign-off happens before go-live.

No single view works. Compose them by altitude.

Each format wins at one altitude and fails at the others. The pattern that scales is a ladder: start from a question, show the smallest view that answers it, and let every element open the next level down.

Design principles

Start from the change, not the org.Search or pick a component and the map scopes itself around it.
Cap at two hops by default.Show what lies beyond as a count, not as more dots.
Aggregate hubs.Account and Opportunity collapse to one node with a count until opened.
Rank before you render.A list sorted by severity and owner sits beside every graph.
Type the edges.Writes, reads, and syncs-to carry different risk and should look different.
Keep positions stable.Same component, same place, every visit, so a mental map can form.
